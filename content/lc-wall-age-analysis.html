Stone wall age analysis in Little Compton

SEP 2024 / NOTE
This journal entry explores a side-project of my archaeometry work in Rhode Island.

Historical land use patterns, in many instances, shape our modern world. Over time, properties are sold, subdivided, and rezoned. Likewise, structures are demolished, built, or reimagined. Some structures are more permanent. Many modern property boundaries in Rhode Island and elsewhere in New England can be roughly marked by stone walls built hundreds of years ago.

One recurring question when it comes to studying stone walls is their age. We know roughly when the height of stone wall construction in New England was (the early 1800s), but how can we figure out what the oldest parts of a network are? If agricultural stone walls were built as "linear landfills," as they have been referred to by Dr. Robert Thorson, it follows that wall construction for this purpose must have begun early on in New England's farming history. In other words, the natural processes governing how stones travel through the soil has always been present, and it couldn't have taken more than one harvest for those early pioneers to figure out what to do with them.

(Left) Little Compton, as planned in the 1600s (map courtesy of the Little Compton Historical Society, and georeferenced by me); (right) extant stone walls as delineated by the Rhode Island Stone Wall Mapping Project.

My research on stone walls has frequently taken me to the town of Little Compton, Rhode Island. A representative of their historical society provided me with a 1600s property map of the town, and I began to think of how it could be used to answer the stone wall age question.

What emerged was an interesting, albeit shaky, guess as to where the oldest walls exist. As permanent as they appear today, during the period that they were built stone walls were frequently moved around. The old map, while mostly accurate, suffers from some dimensional problems. Even after georeferencing it, some inaccuracies were exaggerated. That said, I think the results are interesting. The most overlaps exist in the central and western areas of the town. This suggests that the types of agriculture taking place in those areas produced more problem stones than elsewhere. I have it on good authority that eastern Little Compton had large woodlots, and perhaps that has something to do with it.

(Top) Comparison of allotment lines (red) derived from the old map versus stone walls derived from the Rhode Island Stone Wall Mapping Project (black). (Bottom) Candidates for "oldest walls" in Little Compton, based on how well current stone walls align with the 1600s allotment lines.

Regardless, I think this idea can be expanded upon given the right data. I attempted to reproduce this analysis for Block Island (New Shoreham), but the old property maps that I found from this era were much more innacurate. Not for lack of trying, I was wholly unsuccessful in georeferencing them.
