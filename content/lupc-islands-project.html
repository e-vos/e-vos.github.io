Coastal Resilience Planning for Rural Maine Islands

2024 / STUDY
This project was performed while working with the Maine Land Use Planning Commission (LUPC), a state planning agency that serves as the planning and zoning authority for the unorganized and deorganized territories of Maine. Using my data, the coastal resilience work being done by the LUPC has been featured in state government publications and news media, including the Maine Public Broadcasting Network and Bangor Daily News.
A typical coastal scene in Penobscot Bay, where several LUPC islands are situated (photo by Elliot Vosburgh, 2023).

Problem: Data Gaps in the Gulf of Maine

The LUPC oversees hundreds of islands, yet the specific risks to island infrastructure posed by sea level changes remain largely unquantified. With sea levels projected to rise by up to 7 feet by 2100, the LUPC required a granular, structure-level analysis to enrich the data available for these landmasses.

Solution: Multi-scale Risk Framework

I developed a geospatial workflow to build a comprehensive inventory of structures within the LUPC's coastal island service area. This included determining which islands contained structures, digitizing structure locations, and assessing structure vulnerability based on NOAA sea level rise projections. My analysis encompassed all 319 LUPC coastal islands, and produced data for 838 individual structures. The resulting dataset was the first of its kind for the region.

Methodology

Two parallel analyses were performed using Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), NAIP imagery, and NOAA sea-level rise (SLR) projections:

1. Inundation modeling: I developed a batch-processing workflow to calculate land area loss across 10 distinct SLR scenarios. This produced a unique inundation dataset for each island.

2. Structure metrics: I calculated the elevation, coastline proximity, and SLR overlap for every structure. To produce the structure dataset, I performed two analyses using LiDAR data and NAIP imagery, respectively. First, a series of manipulations of the LiDAR data were done to extract points whose spatial qualities matched roughly what single- and two-storey buildings would have. This produced a dataset I could check the results of the subsequent step against. I then manually extracted structures using NAIP imagery by systematically going through each of the 319 islands. By comparing the two datasets, I was able to create a point dataset containing 838 structures.

Significance

The project produced datasets that are actively being used by planners. This information can be used not only for resilience work, but also for understanding the socioeconomic makeup of rural Maine islands. Were similar data to be combined with structure count, and possibly with a structure type dataset (e.g., based on roof size) derived from it, it's possible to estimate certain unknown quantities like population size.

What's more, the spatial layout of island structures allows for the inference of several characteristics. Beyond those related to coastal resilience, it can tell us how land is being utilized in reality, rather than how it appears on paper. It can also show where potential weak links in emergency services exist.
